# Related Work

## Alignment and instruction tuning

The current generation of instruction-tuned LLMs derives its safety behavior largely from reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) and its variants. Ouyang et al. (2022, NeurIPS) established the supervised fine-tuning plus reward-model plus PPO pipeline that produced InstructGPT and remains the canonical recipe behind every model studied here. Bai et al. (2022, Anthropic technical report) introduced Constitutional AI, replacing portions of human preference data with model-generated critiques against a written set of principles, and demonstrated that harmlessness and helpfulness can be jointly optimized rather than purely traded off. Both lines of work assume an *aligned base behavior* into which deployers can then layer additional system-level instructions; the present paper interrogates exactly the value of that additional layer for small open models.

## Jailbreak attacks

The empirical jailbreak literature crystallized around three families of attack. Zou et al. (2023, arXiv) introduced Greedy Coordinate Gradient (GCG) and released AdvBench, a set of harmful behaviors paired with optimized adversarial suffixes that transfer across models; AdvBench is the dataset distributed within the GCG paper rather than a separate publication, and its suffixes have since become a de facto smoke test. Wei et al. (2023, NeurIPS) in "Jailbroken: How Does LLM Safety Training Fail?" decomposed failure modes into *competing objectives* (the model is rewarded for being helpful and for refusing, and adversarial prompts pit these against one another) and *mismatched generalization* (safety training does not cover the distribution of inputs the model actually sees), and instantiated these axes as a battery of 30+ concrete attack templates that have since been reused as evaluation harnesses. Chao et al. (2023, arXiv) proposed PAIR (Prompt Automatic Iterative Refinement), in which an attacker LLM iteratively rewrites a jailbreak prompt against a target, producing semantically natural attacks that bypass perplexity-based filters. Mazeika et al. (2024, ICML) released HarmBench, a standardized harmful-behavior evaluation that unifies attack methods and target behaviors under a single judge, and reported wide variation in robustness across both open and closed models. Chao et al. (2024, NeurIPS D&B) released JailbreakBench, a reproducible harness with a frozen behavior set and a public attack leaderboard, and Souly et al. (2024, NeurIPS D&B) released StrongREJECT, a rubric LLM judge that corrects the common over-counting of empty or uninformative completions as successful jailbreaks. The present study uses HarmBench's *standard behaviors* split plus a small fixed set of canonical GCG suffixes from the public Zou et al. artifact; it does not generate new attacks.

## System-prompt and lightweight defenses

A parallel strand of work asks how much robustness deployers can buy *without* retraining. Inan et al. (2023, Meta technical report) released Llama Guard, an instruction-tuned classifier for input/output moderation; Meta AI subsequently shipped Llama-Guard-2 and Llama-Guard-3 (in 1B and 8B variants), and the smaller Llama-Guard-3-1B model card is what we load as the secondary judge in this paper. Xie et al. (2023, Nature Machine Intelligence) proposed the "self-reminder" defense, a short system-prompt prefix and user-message suffix that reminds the model of its safety obligations; they reported substantial ASR reductions on GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 with minimal utility loss measured on standard NLP benchmarks. Phute et al. (2024, ICLR Tiny Papers) introduced LLM Self Defense, in which a second model (or the same model in a separate call) classifies the candidate response before release. These lightweight defenses are attractive precisely because they require no weight changes and impose no training cost — but the over-refusal tax they levy on benign-but-sensitive queries is rarely quantified in the same study, and the empirical evidence on the ceiling of system-prompt defenses is mixed: Xie et al. and Zhang et al. report large ASR reductions against static attacks, while Andriushchenko et al. show adaptive search reaches 100% ASR on sub-4B targets regardless of the prompt. The safety-helpfulness trade-off curve for stronger system prompts also exhibits non-monotone behaviour in published over-refusal audits, which is one of the reasons we measure FRR jointly with ASR.

## Over-refusal evaluation

The over-refusal literature is the symmetric counterpart to jailbreak evaluation. Röttger et al. (2024, NAACL) released XSTest, 250 safe prompts and 200 unsafe contrast prompts designed to surface *exaggerated safety* — refusals on queries that merely *resemble* unsafe ones (e.g., "how do I kill a Python process"). Cui et al. (2025, ICML) extended this with OR-Bench, an automatically generated suite of ~80,000 seemingly-toxic prompts spanning ten refusal categories, with an "OR-Bench-Hard" subset of 1,319 prompts, and showed that frontier models including GPT-4 and Claude-3 over-refuse on a non-trivial fraction. Han et al. (2024, NeurIPS D&B) released WildGuard, a unified classifier covering harm, jailbreak, and refusal detection that has been adopted as a third-party reference judge for both axes. All three works argue, and we adopt the position, that ASR cannot be interpreted without a paired FRR measurement, because a model can trivially achieve zero ASR by refusing everything.

## Small open instruction-tuned models

The four models we study are the current generation of widely-deployed small open instruction-tuned LLMs. Abdin et al. (2024, Microsoft technical report) released the Phi-3 family, emphasizing data-quality-driven training that yields strong instruction-following at 3.8B parameters; the *mini-4k-instruct* variant is the laptop-deployable workhorse of that family, and Haider et al. (2024, Microsoft technical report) document its safety post-training process. Grattafiori et al. (2024, Meta technical report) released the Llama 3 herd, including the 3.2-3B-Instruct variant we use, which inherits the Llama 3 safety recipe at a substantially smaller compute footprint. Yang et al. (2024, Alibaba technical report) released Qwen2.5, reporting competitive instruction-following and multilingual coverage at the 3B scale. Riviere et al. (2024, Google DeepMind technical report) released Gemma 2, with the 2B-Instruct variant explicitly targeted at on-device deployment. These four models are roughly contemporaneous, all openly licensed for research, and all runnable under 4-bit quantization on a consumer laptop — making them the natural population for a practitioner-facing comparison. We note that the sub-4B class is moving quickly: Phi-4-mini, Qwen3, Llama-3.3, SmolLM2, and OLMo-2 1B have appeared since these four were released; the durable contribution we target is therefore the harness and protocol, instantiated on this 2024 vintage with a refresh appendix planned for any 1-4B model released between submission and camera-ready.

## Positioning of this paper

The published comparative-audit literature is rich in three directions taken largely in isolation: attack construction, defense construction, and over-refusal measurement. The intersection — *a head-to-head measurement of attack success and over-refusal, on small deployable open models, under the cheapest plausible deployer-side defense* — is conspicuously underpopulated. The coverage matrix below makes the gap explicit. We list each comparative-audit precedent and mark which of five axes it covers: sub-4B model class (S4B), joint ASR+FRR on the same models (J), system-prompt intervention measured (SP), judge triangulation with a ranking-stability test (JT), and laptop reproducibility (LR).

The empty intersection that the present paper fills is the column-product (S4B, J, SP, JT, LR): sub-4B model class with joint ASR and FRR on the same models under the same system-prompt intervention, evaluated under at least two independent judges with an explicit ranking-stability test and reproducible on a single laptop. Existing comparisons either fix the model and vary the attack (Mazeika et al., 2024), fix the attack and vary the defense (Xie et al., 2023), or fix the threat model and vary over-refusal categories (Cui et al., 2025). We hold all three near-fixed and vary the *model*, paying the cost of a narrower contribution in exchange for an evaluation grid that maps directly onto the question a deployer actually asks: *given these four candidate small open models and this one simple defense, what do I gain and what do I lose?*
