# 01 — Goals

## Working title

Small Models, Same Rules: A Joint Attack-Success and False-Refusal Audit of Sub-4B Open LLMs Under a Single Defensive System Prompt.

## Problem statement

Sub-4B instruction-tuned open language models — Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct, Phi-3-mini, Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct, and Gemma-2-2B-it — are now the default substrate for on-device assistants, laptop inference stacks (Ollama, llama.cpp), and air-gapped enterprise pilots. These are precisely the deployment contexts in which Responsible AI (RAI) practitioners have the least defensive infrastructure available: no classifier auxiliary, no decoding-time intervention, no representation-engineering layer, and frequently no path to fine-tuning. Yet the jailbreak-evaluation literature has developed in the opposite direction. HarmBench, JailbreakBench, and StrongREJECT concentrate on 7B+ open models and frontier closed models. XSTest, OR-Bench, and PHTest measure false-refusal rate (FRR) but rarely on the same systems for which attack-success rate (ASR) is reported. The dominant defenses studied — SmoothLLM, RPO, circuit breakers, SafeDecoding, Llama-Guard pipelines — assume an inference stack most practitioners cannot modify. The single intervention practitioners actually deploy, a natural-language defensive system prompt in the spirit of Xie et al.'s self-reminder and Zhang et al.'s goal-prioritization work, has not been audited head-to-head on the sub-4B open class with both ASR and FRR measured jointly. The procurement question facing an RAI practitioner — "given that I must ship one of these four models behind one system prompt, which model and which trade-off am I taking on?" — has no published answer.

## Objectives

1. **Deliver a reproducible, laptop-runnable evaluation harness** for the four target sub-4B open models, with pinned Hugging Face revisions, pinned chat templates, pinned benchmark commits, and a single shell command that reproduces every table and figure in the paper on a consumer 16 GB GPU in under 18 GPU-hours.

2. **Report a joint (ASR, FRR) frontier** for all four models under empty and defensive system prompts, using HarmBench's 200-behavior standard set (filtered to the standard functional category and audited at load time against the published HarmBench semantic-category histogram) with the option to append ten canonical pre-computed GCG suffixes loaded at runtime from the public Zou et al. artifact, and using XSTest-250 with the OR-Bench-Hard subset (1,319 prompts) for over-refusal measurement, so that the safety-helpfulness trade-off is visible on one chart rather than split across two papers.

3. **Triangulate ASR across multiple judges.** Our primary implemented judges are the HarmBench Llama-2-13B fine-tuned classifier (`cais/HarmBench-Llama-2-13b-cls`, loaded 4-bit via bitsandbytes when the GPU permits, with the HarmBench Mistral-7B classifier `cais/HarmBench-Mistral-7b-val-cls` as a smaller substitute when it does not) and Llama-Guard-3-1B (`meta-llama/Llama-Guard-3-1B`) as the laptop-feasible safety classifier. The deliberately weak refusal-keyword baseline is retained as a methodological floor and as an independent third signal for triangulation; it is never used as a fallback inside another judge. We report a Kendall's tau ranking-stability analysis with a pre-registered rule that any pairwise model ranking flip across judges is flagged in the main text. The choice between the 13B and the 7B HarmBench classifier is documented in Appendix A and pinned to a Hugging Face commit hash; the 8B variant of Llama-Guard-3 is documented as a future-work upgrade once the harness is run on >=24 GB VRAM hardware.

4. **Quantify defense cost**, defined as the ratio of XSTest compliance lost to HarmBench behaviors blocked, for each model under the identical 60-word defensive system prompt, and test whether this cost is uniform across the four models or whether a single organisational safety policy produces differential helpfulness regressions across the open-model fleet.

5. **Decompose attack-family vulnerability** across HarmBench's seven semantic categories per model, separating safety that derives from alignment from safety that derives from limited capability (for example, cipher-based attacks failing because sub-4B models cannot decode the cipher), and discuss the implication that capability growth will erode the latter component.

6. **Establish judge and prompt sensitivity bounds** through pre-registered defensive prompt variants (terse, primary, constitutional-style), two temperature regimes (T = 0 deterministic and T = 0.7 best-of-five), Wilson 95% confidence intervals on every reported rate, McNemar paired tests for before/after defense within model, and bootstrap (B = 1000) intervals on ASR-difference distributions.

7. **Produce a one-page practitioner checklist** for auditing a sub-4B open model before deployment, derived directly from the empirical findings, and release it alongside the harness so that the contribution is operational rather than archival.

## Contribution at multiple levels

For the **RAI practitioner**, the contribution is a deployable audit kit: pick a model, paste the defensive system prompt, run one command, read the joint frontier, and verify the ranking under at least two judges before shipping. For the **methods community**, the contribution is the explicit demonstration that single-judge, single-axis ASR reporting is insufficient for procurement claims in the small-model class; we expect at least one ranking flip across judges and report it openly per the pre-registration. For the **FAccT and AIES communities**, the contribution is an audit-not-capability paper that proposes no new attack and no new defense, fitting the venues' preference for measurement, evaluation, and accountability work. For the **on-device deployment community**, the contribution is the first head-to-head sub-4B comparison under a unified protocol, filling a gap that HarmBench and JailbreakBench have left open as their evaluation effort has concentrated on larger models. The aggregated effect is a paper that any reader — whether researcher, reviewer, or deployment engineer — can read and act on within the constraints they actually face.

## Ethics

The paper uses only previously published, publicly licensed benchmark prompts (HarmBench, JailbreakBench, AdvBench-derived GCG suffixes, XSTest, OR-Bench) and previously released open-weight models pulled at pinned revisions. No new attack is developed; no GCG, PAIR, or TAP re-run is performed; no new adversarial artifact is produced or released. Harmful model completions are summarised only in aggregate statistics and judge labels; no harmful completion is reproduced verbatim in the paper, supplementary material, or public release artifact. By default the harness persists only per-prompt judge labels and aggregate statistics, not raw completions. An opt-in encrypted-completions sink is available for reviewer-audit purposes, with completions written via Fernet symmetric encryption to a path inside the author's Microsoft-managed environment under a bounded retention policy; the key-management and access protocol is documented in Appendix E. A responsible-disclosure procedure is followed: a pre-print draft is shared with the four model providers (Meta, Microsoft, Google DeepMind, Alibaba) and the four benchmark/judge maintainers 14-30 days before public release, and the contact dates are logged in Appendix I alongside the public pre-registration (a Git tag in the project repository). Author conflict of interest is declared: the author is affiliated with Microsoft, and Phi-3-mini is a Microsoft model; mitigations are public pre-registration of the protocol (committed and tagged in the public repository before any run), identical treatment of all four models, judge triangulation, and a pre-commitment that if Phi-3-mini is the safety-frontier model under any judge we report the result with the same emphasis as for any other model, explicitly attempt to find a judge or condition under which it is not, and include the negative-result attempt in the paper. The author follows the Microsoft Responsible AI Standard throughout, and benchmark licenses (HarmBench: MIT for code; behavior text aggregated under the HarmBench repository terms with provenance to component datasets; JailbreakBench JBB-Behaviors MIT; XSTest CC-BY-4.0; OR-Bench CC-BY-4.0; AdvBench distributed under the GCG MIT-licensed repository, with provenance to prior harmful-behavior corpora noted) are verified against each dataset's LICENSE file and tracked in the Appendix E compliance checklist.

## Fit with the author

The author is a Microsoft RAI practitioner whose prior published work has used the comparative-audit pattern: pick an existing problem, apply a unified protocol, compare against the existing literature, and report what changes. The paper fits that pattern exactly. It does not require a research-engineering platform: the full grid runs overnight on a consumer GPU, no paid APIs are used, and the harness depends only on open weights and public benchmarks. The work also fits the author's institutional position. As an RAI practitioner rather than a capability researcher, audit and measurement contributions sit naturally within the author's remit, and the deliverable — a practitioner checklist plus an open harness — is the kind of artifact RAI organisations can adopt directly. The venue targets, arXiv preprint followed by ACM FAccT 2027 with IEEE TAI as a fallback, match both the contribution type and the author's prior comparative-style publication record.
